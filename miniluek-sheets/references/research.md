# Research behind the preset

Each row: the finding, then where it shaped the sheets. Quotes are from the sources' abstracts or
full texts. No study has tested miniLÜK or these sheets; each design choice borrows evidence from
studies of the underlying skill.

## Progression and models

| Source | Finding | In the sheets |
|---|---|---|
| Frye et al. 2013, *Teaching Math to Young Children*, IES/What Works Clearinghouse practice guide (NCEE 2014-4005) | Recommendation 1 (moderate evidence): "provide opportunities for children to practice recognizing the total number of objects in small collections (one to three items) … Next, promote accurate one-to-one counting … use number words and counting to compare quantities … label collections with number words and numerals … then basic problems" | overall order S1 → S13 |
| Krajewski & Schneider 2009, *J Exp Child Psychol* 103:516–531 | Quantity-number competence in three levels (number words without quantity; exact quantity–number links; part-whole relations); at age 5 it predicted math achievement in grade 3 | S2–S11 exact links, S12 part-whole |
| Nelson & McMaster 2019, *J Educ Psychol* (meta-analysis, 33 studies, g = 0.63) | Concrete-representational-abstract framing and one-to-one counting content went with larger effects; in the relations domain, ordinal numbers and set comparison too. Interventions built on pictures (g 0.58 vs 0.84) or games (0.51 vs 0.75) averaged smaller effects (subgroup comparisons) | objects first, sheets as practice; S2; three comparison sheets |
| Akther, Powell & Lariviere 2025, *Learn Disabil Res Pract* 40:162–180 | Counting-focused interventions in preschool and kindergarten: "moderate-to-strong proximal effects and positive transfer"; modelling, guided practice, feedback and multiple representations were the most common strategies | worked examples, pattern check, redo with objects |
| Spooner et al. 2019, *Remedial Spec Educ* | For students with moderate and severe developmental disabilities: systematic and explicit instruction, manipulatives and graphic organizers are evidence-based practices in math | objects first, fixed format |

## Small sets, counting, patterns

| Source | Finding | In the sheets |
|---|---|---|
| Clements, Sarama & MacDonald 2019, in *Constructing Number* | "Subitizing is the direct perceptual apprehension and identification of the numerosity of a small group of items" | S1 |
| Mix 2008, *Cogn Dev* 23:191–203 | When object similarity competes with number, children make fewer number matches; "only children who understood the number words for the target sets performed above chance" | S1 same objects, S2 different objects; the adult says the number word |
| Starkey & McCandliss 2014, *J Exp Child Psychol* 126:120–137 | "Kindergarten children showed no evidence of grouping structure on enumeration"; the grouping advantage appears from first grade | dice and finger patterns (4–6) after counting |
| Fuson 1988, *Children's Counting and Concepts of Number*, ch. 4 | Object arrangement affects counting correspondence errors | S8 scattered sets |
| Poletti et al. 2025, *Child Development* | Finger-counting training raised addition accuracy from 37.3 % to 77.1 % in 5–6-year-olds (control 39.6 % to 47.8 %), replicated twice | S4 |
| Kullberg et al. 2020, *Educ Stud Math* | Finger patterns that show part-part-whole relations improved addition and subtraction in 5-year-olds | S4, S12 |
| Sarnecka & Carey 2008, *Cognition* 108:662–674 | Children can count and answer "how many" before they understand that the next number word means one more | S13 last |

## Comparing and the number line

| Source | Finding | In the sheets |
|---|---|---|
| Halberda & Feigenson 2008, *Dev Psychol* 44:1457–1465 | Number discrimination sharpens through childhood; 4-year-olds still perform well near ratios 1.25–1.5, 3-year-olds fail below 1.25 | S5 at 2 : 1 and more, S10 at 1.5–2 |
| Leibovich et al. 2017, *Behav Brain Sci* | Continuous magnitudes (area, size) are processed together with number | dots of one size; noted as a limit |
| Schneider et al. 2017, *Dev Sci* (meta-analysis, 45 articles, 17,201 participants) | Comparing numerals correlates with mathematical competence r = .30, comparing dot sets r = .24 | S11 |
| Jacobson et al. 2011, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 35:431–442 | In 262 adolescents, prenatal alcohol exposure related most to magnitude comparison (choosing the larger of two numbers, proximity judgment), which fully mediated its relation to calculation | comparison three times: S5, S10, S11 |
| Mundy & Gilmore 2009, *J Exp Child Psychol* 103:490–502 | "children can map in both directions between symbolic and nonsymbolic numerical representations" | S6 quantity → numeral, S7 numeral → quantity |
| Siegler & Booth 2004, *Child Dev* 75:428–444 | Number-line estimates move from logarithmic to linear between kindergarten and grade 2; linearity correlates with math achievement | S9 |
| Siegler & Ramani 2008, 2009 | Playing a linear 1–10 number board game improved magnitude knowledge, more than a circular board | S9; a companion board game |

## Method, dose, motivation

| Source | Finding | In the sheets |
|---|---|---|
| MILE: Kable, Coles & Taddeo 2007; Coles, Kable & Taddeo 2009; Kable et al. 2015; Kully-Martens et al. 2018 | 1:1 math tutoring with manipulatives and the Focus/Plan, Act, Reflect routine produced larger gains than comparison groups, maintained at follow-up; a manual for parents alone did not | FAR routine on every sheet; content list (subitizing, counting, cardinality, conservation, magnitude, position, unitizing) |
| Crocker, Riley & Mattson 2014, *Neuropsychology* | Spatial span and spatial recognition memory predicted math achievement | clean page, colour families halve the search |
| Seabrook, Brown & Solity 2005, *Appl Cogn Psychol* 19:107–122 | Distributed teaching of reading beat clustered teaching in a classroom study | one sheet a day, revisit after a week |
| Dose–response meta-analysis, *Educ Psychol Rev* 2025 | Each extra hour of intervention added about .03 effect size units, not significant once moderators were controlled; 1:1 beat small groups | short daily practice, adult 1:1 |
| Cordova & Lepper 1996, *J Educ Psychol* 88:715–730 | Contextualization, personalization and choice raised motivation and learning | theme, the child picks which mastered sheet to repeat |
| Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016, *Perspect Psychol Sci* | No good evidence that working-memory training transfers | no memory drills; quantities stay visible |
