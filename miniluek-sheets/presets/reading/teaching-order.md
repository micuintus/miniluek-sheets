# Teaching order of the reading course

The reading courses are the second course in this skill, after the counting courses. They teach
letters, first sounds, syllables, short printed words. The evidence base is narrower than the counting course's, and the rows in
[research](research.md) carry evidence-type tags. It sits beside this file, shared by both
reading presets.

`reading-readiness.json` is English, `lese-vorbereitung.json` is German. Same sheets,
same tasks, translated titles and goals.

## Number first

Letters teach best once the child can work a LÜK page: 12 numbered tasks, one tile per task,
pattern check at the end. If the counting sheets are not yet mastered, keep them daily and run
reading twice a week, not daily, so the one sheet a day stays the cap.

The German course is the reading route: school teaches reading in German, German is usually the
stronger language, and English basic decoding takes more than twice as long to establish
(Seymour et al. 2003, cited in [research](research.md)). The English course is exposure: it
supports a second spoken language through shows, songs and one parent's time, and it does not
try to alphabetise in two codes at once, which no study has tested at ID-range scores.

## Where to start

Three minutes with the child before the first sheet:

1. **Same or different.** Write A and A, then A and B, on paper: "same or different?"
2. **Name the pictures.** Point at the twelve course pictures one by one ("CAT", "DOG", "FOX", "OTTER", "PIG", "GIFT", "PANDA", "MOUSE", "DAISY", "COOKIE", "BUNNY", "SUN"):
   the adult names each first, then the child repeats. A sheet only starts when the child knows
   the words well enough to repeat them.
3. **First sound.** "CAT starts with k. DOG starts with d. Show me the CAT."

| Result | Start with |
|---|---|
| cannot tell same from different letters | A1, letters only |
| knows most letters | A2 |
| names the letters and repeats the picture words | A3 |

The letters on A1, A2 and A1b are the first letters of the course words (German: B E F G H
K L M P S T W; English: B C D F G H M O P S, plus A and E). The rest of the alphabet is not in the set; the
adult teaches letter names and sounds outside it, in the object step before A3.

## Before each sheet

Each sheet comes after the same idea spoken first: the adult says, claps, sings or points,
then the sheet. Start a sheet when the previous one is mastered, or when the condition below
holds.

| Sheet | First spoken | Start the sheet when |
|---|---|---|
| A1 same letter | letter cards face up: "find the A" | from the start |
| A2 uppercase to lowercase | magnetic letters: "big A, little a" | the child knows most uppercase letters |
| A1b ABC blocks | letter blocks or magnets: "find the block with the same letter" | A1 mastered |
| A3 first sound to picture | "CAT starts with k": show three pictures, the child points | A1 mastered and the picture words are known |
| A3b balloons | three letter cards and a few pictures: "which one starts with this?" | A3 mastered |
| A4 picture to first letter | "which letter does DOG start with?" | A3 mastered |
| A4b garden party | name each of the twelve guests, "which letter does it start with?" | A4 mastered |
| A5 which part it starts with | clap CA-T: "CA-T, the CAT" | A4 mastered (A4b is a review day, not a gate) |
| A6 read the word | sound out CAT together, then the child alone | A5 mastered; "COOKIE-" style longer words only from the second half |
| A6b market day | name each picture, then read three words that differ in one letter: "CAT, CAP or HAT?" | A6 mastered |

A sheet the child cannot do yet goes back until its condition holds. Keep the daily cap:
one sheet a day, reading work sits inside it, never on top of it.

## Picture sheets

Four reading sheets set their 12 badges into one illustration, each after its plain sheet:

| Picture sheet | After | Scene | Task | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1b | A1 | ABC blocks in a toy room | an uppercase letter on a toy block | the same letter |
| A3b | A3 | balloons for the princess | a letter card on a balloon | the picture that starts with it |
| A4b | A4 | the princess's garden party | a guest or a present | its first letter |
| A6b | A6 | market day in a village | a thing to name | its three-letter word |

They are review and transfer: the week-later repeat of the plain sheet, never the daily sheet.
A6b runs the reverse direction of A6, picture to word, and its words share first letters or
rhymes (CAT, CAP, HAT; PEN, HEN; FOX, BOX; German OMA, OPA; TEE, TOR), so only reading the
whole word finds the answer. A story told with a doll carries the motivation, as in the
counting course. Letters
and guests are plain and uniform, each on its own block, balloon or family-coloured halo. No
scene shows a letter outside the twelve stations, and A4b, where pictures are the task, shows
no course picture as decoration. The
plain sheets stay plain throughout the course, so the child learns the format first and the
picture is the reward, not the teaching. Rich familiar objects made preschool counting
harder in one study, and decorated rooms held attention worse in another (see
[research](research.md)); the picture sheets are untested, so a child who miscounts on them
goes back to the plain sheet.

## Design rules

The same rules as the counting course: one direction per sheet, two colour families on matching
sheets so no answer copies a task (`check` refuses it), different-looking task and answer sides,
worked examples per family, feedback by pattern check. English letter names say the letter's
sound, not "see-ay-tee": the adult says the sounds ("kuh-ah-tuh") and slides them together.
German keeps its umlauts everywhere: letter cards, syllable cards, word pills and titles. On
English initial-sound cards the full picture word is captioned under the letter.

The twelve course words start with their letter's sound: CAT, DOG, FOX, OTTER, PIG, GIFT,
PANDA, MOUSE, DAISY, COOKIE, BUNNY, SUN (German: BÄR, KATZE, HUND, FUCHS, ENTE, MAUS,
GESCHENK, SONNE, TORTE, LUTSCHER, WURM, PILZ). German A4 tasks carry the German index
explicitly, because a picture file name would give the English letter (cat would ask for C
instead of K).
